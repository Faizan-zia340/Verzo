## Why formal British English emails are hard to get right

A formal email in British English is not just an email with "please" added. The spelling, the greeting, the sign-off and the level of politeness all send a signal — and getting one of them wrong can make a careful message look careless. Write "organize" instead of "organise", sign off with "Thanks!" to a hiring manager, or open with "Hi" to a solicitor, and the reader notices before they read the second line.

## The 9 email subtypes — pick the right structure first

Penlix offers 9 email subtypes, and each one has its own structure. Picking the right subtype matters more than the wording, because the subtype decides the shape of the email:

- **General** — any ordinary email: subject line, greeting, two or three short paragraphs, one clear request.
- **Cold Outreach** — writing to someone you do not know: a personal subject line, one line showing you know their business, the benefit in two sentences, and a soft request for a short call. Under 150 words.
- **Follow-up** — no reply yet: a reference to the earlier email, a short reminder of the request, a new reason to reply, and a simple ask.
- **Newsletter** — for subscribers: a friendly subject, a warm greeting, two or three sections under headings, and a request to read or reply.
- **Welcome** — for a new subscriber or customer: what happens next, two or three points, and one clear first step.
- **Apology** — when something went wrong: a direct apology in the first line, a short explanation, a plan to fix it, and a concrete remedy. No excuses.
- **Job Application** — subject line with the role and your name, two short paragraphs (why this role, why you are suitable), and a request to view your CV.
- **Thank You** — a sincere thank-you in the first line, one specific thing you are thanking them for, and a short closing line.
- **Meeting Request** — the purpose of the meeting, its importance in two or three sentences, and a request to suggest a time.

Choosing the wrong subtype changes the tone completely — an apology written like a cold outreach is the most common mistake.

## British conventions worth knowing

**Greetings.** "Dear Mr Smith" or "Dear Ms Patel" for formal mail. "Dear Sir or Madam" only when you genuinely do not know the name. "Hi" is for colleagues you already know — never for a first formal email.

**Sign-offs.** The rule is simple: if you opened with a name, close with "Yours sincerely". If you opened with "Dear Sir or Madam", close with "Yours faithfully". In practice, "Kind regards" is now accepted in most professional mail, and "Best regards" is a safe middle ground. Avoid "Cheers" and "Thanks!" in formal mail.

**Spelling.** Penlix handles this when English (UK) is selected, but check the result anyway: organise, colour, favour, behaviour, centre, licence (noun) vs license (verb), cheque (not check) for payment. A single American spelling in an otherwise British email stands out.

**Tone.** British formal English is polite but direct. "I would be grateful if you could…" reads better than "Please do this ASAP". Avoid slang, avoid exclamation marks in formal mail, and keep sentences short — one idea per sentence.

What works here: the subject line states the purpose, the greeting is formal, the request is specific, and the sign-off matches the greeting.

## Common mistakes

1. **Wrong subtype.** Writing a follow-up as if it were a cold outreach, or an apology as a general email — the tone goes wrong before the first sentence.
2. **Vague details.** "Write an email about the job" gives Penlix nothing to work with. Say who, what and what you want — all three.
3. **American spelling slipping in.** One "organize" or "color" in a British email is the first thing a UK reader notices. Generate in English (UK) and check the result.
4. **No subject line check.** Penlix writes the subject line — use it, but read it. A subject line with the wrong name or date is worse than none.
5. **Sending without reading.** Names, dates and links are the three things most likely to be wrong if your details were wrong. Read the draft once before sending.
